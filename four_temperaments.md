# The Four Temperaments: A Comprehensive Academic Analysis

## I. INTRODUCTION AND HISTORICAL OVERVIEW

The Four Temperaments theory represents one of the oldest personality typologies in Western intellectual history, spanning approximately 2,500 years of continuous influence on medicine, philosophy, psychology, and cultural thought. It constitutes a proto-psychological framework that classifies human beings into four fundamental dispositional types based on the predominance of particular bodily fluids (humours), and by extension, characteristic patterns of emotionality, behaviour, and cognitive style.

---

## II. ANCIENT ORIGINS

### A. Pre-Hippocratic Foundations

The philosophical groundwork for the temperament theory lies in **Empedocles of Acragas** (c. 494–434 BCE), who proposed that all matter is composed of four classical elements: **earth, water, fire, and air**. These elements were associated with fundamental qualities arranged in binary pairs:

- **Hot** vs. **Cold**
- **Wet (Moist)** vs. **Dry**

Each element embodied a unique combination:
| Element | Qualities |
|---------|-----------|
| Fire | Hot and Dry |
| Air | Hot and Wet |
| Water | Cold and Wet |
| Earth | Cold and Dry |

This cosmological schema provided the metaphysical architecture upon which the humoral system would be constructed, reflecting the Greek commitment to finding structural correspondences between the macrocosm (the universe) and the microcosm (the human body).

### B. Hippocratic Humoralism

**Hippocrates of Kos** (c. 460–370 BCE) and the authors of the **Hippocratic Corpus**—particularly the treatise *On the Nature of Man* (Περὶ φύσιος ἀνθρώπου), traditionally attributed to Hippocrates' son-in-law **Polybus**—systematised the doctrine of the four humours as a medical theory. The four humours were:

1. **Blood** (haima/sanguis) — produced in the liver, associated with air, hot and moist
2. **Yellow bile** (cholē/cholera) — produced in the liver/gallbladder, associated with fire, hot and dry
3. **Black bile** (melaina cholē/melancholia) — associated with the spleen, associated with earth, cold and dry
4. **Phlegm** (phlegma) — associated with the brain/lungs, associated with water, cold and moist

Health was understood as **eucrasia** (εὐκρασία) — a harmonious balance or proper mixture of the humours — while disease was **dyscrasia** (δυσκρασία), an imbalance. The Hippocratic writers recognised that individuals had constitutional predispositions toward the dominance of particular humours, which influenced susceptibility to certain diseases, seasonal variations in health, and responses to environmental factors.

Importantly, the Hippocratic tradition was primarily **medical** rather than psychological. The temperaments as personality types were a later elaboration.

### C. Galen of Pergamon

The decisive synthesis was achieved by **Claudius Galenus (Galen)** (129–c. 216 CE), the most influential physician of antiquity after Hippocrates. In works including *De Temperamentis* (*On Temperaments/Mixtures*), *De Sanitate Tuenda*, *Quod Animi Mores Corporis Temperamenta Sequantur* (*That the Faculties of the Soul Follow the Temperaments of the Body*), and other writings, Galen accomplished several things:

1. **Formalised the link between humours and personality**: He argued explicitly that the predominant humour in a person's constitution determined their characteristic emotional and behavioural patterns — their *temperamentum* (Latin: "proper mixture").

2. **Named the four temperaments** based on the dominant humour:
   - **Sanguine** (sanguis = blood)
   - **Choleric** (cholē = yellow bile)
   - **Melancholic** (melaina cholē = black bile)
   - **Phlegmatic** (phlegma = phlegm)

3. **Established a psychosomatic framework**: Galen's thesis in *Quod Animi Mores* was remarkably materialist for its era — he argued that the soul's faculties (cognitive, emotional, appetitive) are dependent upon or identical with the body's temperamental mixture, thus making character a function of physiology.

4. **Elaborated nine temperamental types**: Beyond the four "simple" temperaments (dominated by one humour), Galen identified four compound types (dominated by combinations of two qualities) and one ideally balanced type, totalling nine. However, the fourfold scheme dominated subsequent reception.

Galen also connected temperaments to age, sex, season, and geography:
- **Sanguine**: predominant in spring, youth, and warm-moist climates
- **Choleric**: predominant in summer, young adulthood
- **Melancholic**: predominant in autumn, maturity
- **Phlegmatic**: predominant in winter, old age

## III. DETAILED CHARACTERISATION OF THE FOUR TEMPERAMENTS

### A. The Sanguine Temperament

**Dominant humour**: Blood
**Elemental association**: Air
**Qualities**: Hot and Moist
**Organ**: Liver (as blood-producing organ)
**Season**: Spring
**Life stage**: Childhood/Youth

**Psychological profile**:
- **Emotionally**: The sanguine individual is characterised by emotional lability with a positive hedonic tone. Emotions arise quickly and dissipate readily. The sanguine person is cheerful, optimistic, enthusiastic, and socially warm, but may be superficial in emotional attachments. There is a tendency toward pleasure-seeking and a low threshold for boredom.
- **Socially**: Highly sociable, talkative, charismatic, and interpersonally engaging. The sanguine temperament inclines toward extraversion, gregariousness, and a desire for novelty and social stimulation. They tend to be persuasive, entertaining, and popular.
- **Cognitively**: Quick but often shallow in intellectual processing. The sanguine mind tends toward breadth rather than depth, showing curiosity and versatility but potentially lacking sustained concentration and systematic rigour.
- **Behavioural tendencies**: Impulsive, spontaneous, restless, and distractible. Prone to inconsistency and unreliability. May struggle with sustained effort, follow-through, and self-discipline.
- **Vices associated**: Hedonism, vanity, superficiality, inconstancy, gluttony, lust, lack of seriousness.
- **Virtues associated**: Generosity, warmth, hopefulness, resilience, adaptability, social bonding.
- **Pathological extremes**: Mania, hysterical states, excessive indulgence, dissipation.

**Physical appearance** (in traditional characterisations): Ruddy complexion, warm skin, robust and fleshy build, bright or lively eyes, tendency toward corpulence.

### B. The Choleric Temperament

**Dominant humour**: Yellow bile
**Elemental association**: Fire
**Qualities**: Hot and Dry
**Organ**: Liver/Gallbladder
**Season**: Summer
**Life stage**: Young adulthood/Prime

**Psychological profile**:
- **Emotionally**: The choleric is characterised by intense, quickly aroused emotions — particularly anger, irritability, and passionate engagement. Emotions are strong but not necessarily long-lasting (though grudges may be held). There is a marked tendency toward decisiveness and forceful expression.
- **Socially**: Dominant, assertive, commanding, and often confrontational. Cholerics are natural leaders who seek authority and control. They are direct, sometimes to the point of abrasiveness, and have little patience for perceived weakness, incompetence, or delay.
- **Cognitively**: Practical, decisive, goal-oriented, and strategic. The choleric mind excels at executive function, planning, and action-oriented thinking. However, it may be prone to hasty judgement, dogmatism, and intolerance of ambiguity.
- **Behavioural tendencies**: Energetic, ambitious, competitive, industrious, and driven. The choleric is action-oriented and task-focused, often at the expense of interpersonal sensitivity. Prone to overwork, aggression, and tyrannical behaviour.
- **Vices associated**: Wrath, pride, impatience, cruelty, tyranny, vindictiveness, arrogance.
- **Virtues associated**: Courage, determination, leadership, decisiveness, industriousness, justice (in the punitive sense).
- **Pathological extremes**: Violent rages, paranoid states, inflammatory diseases, fevers.

**Physical appearance**: Lean, muscular, angular features, yellowish or sallow complexion, sharp eyes, tendency toward thinness and wiry build.

### C. The Melancholic Temperament

**Dominant humour**: Black bile
**Elemental association**: Earth
**Qualities**: Cold and Dry
**Organ**: Spleen
**Season**: Autumn
**Life stage**: Maturity/Middle age

**Psychological profile**:
- **Emotionally**: The melancholic temperament is characterised by deep, sustained, and often sombre emotional states. Emotions are slow to arise but long-lasting and profoundly felt. There is a constitutional tendency toward sadness, anxiety, fearfulness, and introspective brooding. However, the melancholic also possesses an exceptional capacity for emotional depth and aesthetic sensitivity.
- **Socially**: Introverted, reserved, often solitary or selective in social relationships. Melancholics tend to be loyal and devoted in close relationships but may appear distant, awkward, or critical in broader social contexts. They are often perfectionistic in their expectations of themselves and others.
- **Cognitively**: Analytical, detail-oriented, systematic, and reflective. The melancholic mind excels at sustained, deep thought, critical analysis, and careful observation. There is a strong association with intellectual and artistic achievement, as elaborated in the **Problem XXX.1** tradition (discussed below). However, this type is also prone to rumination, obsessive thinking, and cognitive rigidity.
- **Behavioural tendencies**: Cautious, methodical, orderly, and conscientious. Melancholics are often perfectionists who maintain high standards. They may struggle with decisiveness, being prone to analysis paralysis. Habitual and routine-oriented.
- **Vices associated**: Envy, avarice, despondency, misanthropy, excessive self-criticism, rigidity, suspiciousness.
- **Virtues associated**: Wisdom, depth, faithfulness, conscientiousness, artistic sensitivity, moral seriousness.
- **Pathological extremes**: Clinical depression (melancholia proper), hypochondria, obsessive-compulsive tendencies, paranoia, delusions.

**Physical appearance**: Dark complexion, thin and angular build, prominent veins, dark eyes, gaunt features.

#### Special Note: The *Problemata* XXX.1 and Melancholic Genius

The pseudo-Aristotelian text *Problemata* (Book XXX, Problem 1) — likely composed by a Peripatetic author, possibly **Theophrastus** — posed the famous question: "Why is it that all men who have become outstanding in philosophy, statesmanship, poetry, or the arts are melancholic?" This text established a lasting association between melancholy and genius, creativity, and exceptional ability. It conceptualised black bile as a substance analogous to wine, which, depending on its temperature and proportion, could produce states ranging from torpid depression to inspired ecstasy. This idea profoundly influenced Renaissance thought (through Marsilio Ficino and others) and remained central to Romantic conceptions of the artist.

### D. The Phlegmatic Temperament

**Dominant humour**: Phlegm
**Elemental association**: Water
**Qualities**: Cold and Moist
**Organ**: Brain/Lungs
**Season**: Winter
**Life stage**: Old age

**Psychological profile**:
- **Emotionally**: The phlegmatic temperament is characterised by emotional stability, equanimity, and low reactivity. Emotions are muted, slow to arise, and expressed with restraint. The phlegmatic individual is typically calm, placid, patient, and unperturbed by circumstances that would agitate other types. However, this emotional dampening can shade into apathy, indifference, and emotional flatness.
- **Socially**: Agreeable, accommodating, diplomatic, and conflict-averse. Phlegmatics are often pleasant and easy to be around but may lack initiative in social interactions. They are good listeners and mediators but may be passive, yielding, and reluctant to assert themselves.
- **Cognitively**: Steady, deliberate, observational, and judicious. The phlegmatic mind processes information slowly but thoroughly. There is a capacity for patience and objectivity but a potential deficit in motivation, curiosity, and intellectual ambition.
- **Behavioural tendencies**: Passive, routine-oriented, unhurried, and resistant to change. The phlegmatic type is reliable and consistent but may be sluggish, lazy, and unmotivated. They tend to avoid exertion and prefer comfort and stability.
- **Vices associated**: Sloth, cowardice, apathy, indecisiveness, passivity, dullness.
- **Virtues associated**: Patience, reliability, peacefulness, diplomacy, steadfastness, contentment.
- **Pathological extremes**: Lethargy, excessive sleepiness, oedema, catarrhal conditions, general enervation.

**Physical appearance**: Pale, cool skin, soft and rounded features, tendency toward corpulence, dull eyes, sluggish movements.

## IV. SYSTEMATIC CORRESPONDENCES

The temperaments were embedded within a rich system of analogical correspondences characteristic of pre-modern cosmology:

| Category | Sanguine | Choleric | Melancholic | Phlegmatic |
|----------|----------|----------|-------------|------------|
| **Humour** | Blood | Yellow bile | Black bile | Phlegm |
| **Element** | Air | Fire | Earth | Water |
| **Quality pair** | Hot & Moist | Hot & Dry | Cold & Dry | Cold & Moist |
| **Season** | Spring | Summer | Autumn | Winter |
| **Time of day** | Morning | Midday | Evening | Night |
| **Life stage** | Childhood | Youth | Maturity | Old age |
| **Planet** | Jupiter | Mars | Saturn | Moon (or Venus) |
| **Organ** | Liver | Gallbladder | Spleen | Brain/Lungs |
| **Colour** | Red | Yellow | Black | White/Pale |
| **Taste** | Sweet | Bitter | Sour/Acrid | Salty/Bland |
| **Cardinal direction** | South (or East) | East (or South) | West | North |

This system of correspondences served as a cognitive framework for understanding the natural world as an interconnected whole, where the same structural principles operated at every level of reality.

## V. MEDIEVAL TRANSMISSION AND ELABORATION

### A. Islamic Medicine

The Galenic tradition was preserved, translated, and significantly elaborated by scholars writing in Arabic during the Islamic Golden Age. Key figures include:

- **Hunayn ibn Ishaq** (Johannitius, 809–873): Translated Galen's works into Arabic; his *Isagoge* (*Kitab al-Mudkhal fi al-Tibb*) became a foundational medical textbook that systematised temperamental theory.
- **Ali ibn al-Abbas al-Majusi** (Haly Abbas, d. 994): In *Kitab Kamil al-Sina'a al-Tibbiyya* (*The Complete Art of Medicine*), provided detailed discussions of temperamental types and their medical implications.
- **Ibn Sina (Avicenna)** (980–1037): In *Al-Qanun fi al-Tibb* (*The Canon of Medicine*), Avicenna gave the most systematic and influential medieval account of temperamental theory. He distinguished between innate temperament (al-mizaj al-asli) and acquired temperament, elaborated the interaction between temperament and environment, and integrated temperamental theory with a sophisticated pharmacology in which drugs were classified by the same quality-pairs (hot/cold, moist/dry) and prescribed to correct humoral imbalances.
- **Ishaq ibn Imran** and **Constantinus Africanus**: Contributed to the medical literature on melancholia specifically.

### B. Latin Scholastic Medicine

With the translation movement of the 11th–13th centuries (particularly through centres like Toledo, Salerno, and Montpellier), Galenic-Avicennian humoralism became the foundation of European university medicine. Key developments:

- The **Schola Medica Salernitana** produced the *Regimen Sanitatis Salernitanum*, a widely circulated verse text that included mnemonic descriptions of the four temperaments.
- **Bartholomaeus Anglicus** (*De Proprietatibus Rerum*, c. 1240) and other encyclopaedists incorporated temperamental theory into comprehensive accounts of natural philosophy.
- Scholastic theologians, particularly **Thomas Aquinas**, addressed the relationship between temperament and moral agency. Aquinas acknowledged that temperament disposed individuals toward certain passions and vices but insisted that rational will and divine grace could overcome temperamental inclinations — thus preserving moral responsibility within a framework that acknowledged constitutional differences.

### C. Iconographic Tradition

The four temperaments became a major subject of visual representation in medieval and Renaissance art. Notable examples include:
- **Illuminated manuscripts** depicting the four temperamental types with characteristic physiognomy, occupations, and symbols
- **Calendar illustrations** linking temperaments to seasons and zodiacal signs
- The influence on **physiognomic treatises** connecting external appearance to internal character
- **Albrecht Dürer's** *Melencolia I* (1514) as the supreme artistic meditation on the melancholic temperament and its association with creative genius

## VI. RENAISSANCE DEVELOPMENTS

### A. Marsilio Ficino and the Rehabilitation of Melancholy

**Marsilio Ficino** (1433–1499), the Florentine Neoplatonist, drew upon the pseudo-Aristotelian *Problemata* XXX.1 and Neoplatonic cosmology to construct a elaborate theory of melancholic genius in *De Vita Libri Tres* (*Three Books on Life*, 1489). Ficino:
- Identified Saturn as the planet of contemplation and philosophical depth
- Argued that scholars and intellectuals were constitutionally melancholic and therefore required specific dietetic and magical regimens to manage their temperament
- Transformed melancholy from a primarily pathological category into a sign of intellectual and spiritual distinction
- Influenced subsequent Renaissance and Baroque conceptions of the artist, poet, and scholar as inherently melancholic figures

### B. Robert Burton

**Robert Burton's** *The Anatomy of Melancholy* (1621) represents the most exhaustive pre-modern treatment of a single temperament. Across its massive, digressive, encyclopaedic structure, Burton catalogued the causes, symptoms, and remedies of melancholy in all its varieties (head melancholy, hypochondriacal melancholy, love melancholy, religious melancholy), drawing upon the full breadth of classical, medieval, and contemporary medical and literary sources.

### C. Theatrical and Literary Uses

The temperaments deeply influenced literary characterisation:
- **Ben Jonson's** "comedy of humours" (*Every Man in His Humour*, 1598; *Every Man out of His Humour*, 1599) explicitly structured dramatic character around humoral imbalance
- **Shakespeare** drew extensively on humoral psychology (e.g., Hamlet as melancholic, Hotspur as choleric, Falstaff as sanguine)
- The temperaments provided a shared vocabulary for discussing character across European literature well into the 18th century

## VII. DECLINE OF HUMORALISM AND SURVIVAL OF TEMPERAMENTAL TYPOLOGY

### A. The Decline of Humoral Medicine

The humoural physiological theory underlying the temperaments was progressively dismantled by developments in anatomy, physiology, and chemistry from the 16th century onward:

- **Andreas Vesalius** (*De Humani Corporis Fabrica*, 1543) challenged Galenic anatomy
- **William Harvey's** demonstration of the circulation of the blood (1628) undermined the Galenic model of blood production and distribution
- **Iatrochemistry** (Paracelsus, van Helmont) and **iatromechanics** (Borelli, Boerhaave) offered alternative physiological frameworks
- **Pathological anatomy** (Morgagni, Bichat) localised disease in organs and tissues rather than in humoral imbalances
- **Cell theory** (Virchow) in the 19th century completed the displacement

By the early 19th century, humoralism was no longer tenable as a medical theory. However, the **phenomenological descriptions** of temperamental types — the personality profiles — survived independently of their humoral rationale.

### B. Kant's Philosophical Treatment

**Immanuel Kant** (1724–1804), in his *Anthropologie in pragmatischer Hinsicht* (*Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View*, 1798), provided an influential philosophical reformulation of the four temperaments. Kant:

- Divided the temperaments along two axes:
  - **Temperaments of feeling** (sanguine and melancholic) vs. **temperaments of activity** (choleric and phlegmatic)
  - Alternatively: temperaments characterised by intensity of arousal vs. duration of arousal
- Described each temperament with characteristic precision:
  - **Sanguine**: "The person of sanguine temperament is carefree and full of hope; attributes great importance to whatever he may be dealing with at the moment, but may have forgotten all about it the next."
  - **Choleric**: "The person of choleric temperament is said to be hot-headed, quickly roused but easily calmed down if the opponent gives in; he is annoyed without hatred."
  - **Melancholic**: "The person who is disposed to melancholy... attributes great importance to all things that concern him, everywhere finds cause for anxiety, and first directs his attention to difficulties."
  - **Phlegmatic**: Kant unusually defended this temperament, arguing that phlegm, understood not as sluggishness but as the tendency to act from principle with constancy and endurance, could constitute a form of practical wisdom.

- Argued that temperaments describe the **form** of emotional and volitional life, not its **content** — they tell us how a person feels and acts, not what they believe or value.

- Suggested that an individual possesses **one** dominant temperament (temperaments do not mix) — a position that contrasts with Galen's allowance for mixed types.

### C. Wundt's Dimensional Analysis

**Wilhelm Wundt** (1832–1920), the founder of experimental psychology, reinterpreted the four temperaments in terms of two independent dimensions:

1. **Strength of emotion** (strong vs. weak)
2. **Speed of change** (changeable/quick vs. unchangeable/slow)

This yielded:
| | Strong emotion | Weak emotion |
|---|---|---|
| **Changeable** | Choleric | Sanguine |
| **Unchangeable** | Melancholic | Phlegmatic |

This dimensional reinterpretation was significant because it moved the temperaments from a categorical (typological) framework toward a **dimensional** one, anticipating later factor-analytic approaches to personality.

## VIII. MODERN SCIENTIFIC ENGAGEMENT

### A. Pavlov's Nervous System Types

**Ivan Pavlov** (1849–1936) proposed that individual differences in higher nervous activity could be classified by three properties of the nervous system:

1. **Strength** of excitatory and inhibitory processes
2. **Equilibrium** (balance between excitation and inhibition)
3. **Mobility** (speed of alternation between excitation and inhibition)

Pavlov explicitly mapped his four nervous system types onto the classical temperaments:

| Pavlov's Type | Properties | Classical Temperament |
|---|---|---|
| Strong, balanced, mobile | High strength, good equilibrium, high mobility | **Sanguine** |
| Strong, unbalanced (excitation predominates) | High strength, poor equilibrium (excitation > inhibition), variable mobility | **Choleric** |
| Strong, balanced, inert | High strength, good equilibrium, low mobility | **Phlegmatic** |
| Weak | Low strength, poor equilibrium, low mobility | **Melancholic** |

This constituted an attempt to provide a **neurophysiological** basis for temperamental differences, grounding the ancient typology in measurable properties of the central nervous system. Pavlov's work was enormously influential in Soviet/Russian psychology and stimulated extensive research programs.

### B. Eysenck's Biological Personality Theory

**Hans Jürgen Eysenck** (1916–1997) developed perhaps the most scientifically rigorous modern framework that explicitly engaged with the four temperaments. Through factor analysis of personality questionnaires and experimental studies, Eysenck identified two (later three) major dimensions of personality:

1. **Extraversion–Introversion (E)**: reflecting individual differences in cortical arousal (introverts being chronically more aroused, thus seeking less external stimulation)
2. **Neuroticism–Emotional Stability (N)**: reflecting individual differences in the reactivity of the limbic system/autonomic nervous system

When these two dimensions are crossed, they reproduce the four classical temperaments:

| | **Stable (Low N)** | **Unstable (High N)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Extravert (High E)** | **Sanguine** | **Choleric** |
| **Introvert (Low E)** | **Phlegmatic** | **Melancholic** |

Eysenck explicitly acknowledged this correspondence and presented it in diagrammatic form in multiple publications (*The Structure of Human Personality*, 1953; *The Biological Basis of Personality*, 1967; *Personality and Individual Differences*, 1985).

Eysenck's contribution was to provide:
- **Psychometric operationalisation** through validated questionnaires (MPI, EPI, EPQ)
- **Biological causal theories** (Ascending Reticular Activating System for E; visceral brain/limbic system for N)
- **Experimental evidence** from studies of conditioning, sensory thresholds, stimulant/depressant drug effects, and other paradigms
- A **dimensional rather than typological** framework, where temperaments are regions in continuous two-dimensional space rather than discrete categories

Eysenck later added a third dimension, **Psychoticism (P)** (tough-mindedness, asociality, impulsivity in a broader sense), which does not map neatly onto the classical system.

### C. Contemporary Temperament Research

Modern developmental and personality psychology uses "temperament" in a related but technically distinct sense — referring to biologically-based individual differences in emotional reactivity, self-regulation, and attentional processes that emerge early in life. Key contemporary frameworks include:

#### 1. Thomas and Chess (1956, 1977)
**Alexander Thomas** and **Stella Chess** identified nine dimensions of infant temperament through the **New York Longitudinal Study (NYLS)**, clustering them into three broad types: **easy**, **difficult**, and **slow-to-warm-up** children. While not directly mapping onto the classical four temperaments, this work reestablished temperament as a legitimate scientific construct.

#### 2. Buss and Plomin (1975, 1984)
**Arnold Buss** and **Robert Plomin** defined temperament as heritable personality traits present from early childhood, identifying three key dimensions: **Emotionality**, **Activity**, and **Sociability (EAS)**. This framework connects to the classical system insofar as emotionality relates to neuroticism and sociability to extraversion.

#### 3. Kagan's Behavioural Inhibition (1989, 1994)
**Jerome Kagan** identified a dimension of **behavioural inhibition** in young children, distinguishing "inhibited" children (who react to novelty with fear and withdrawal) from "uninhibited" children (who approach novelty with curiosity and boldness). Kagan linked this dimension to differences in amygdalar reactivity. The inhibited type has evident affinities with the melancholic temperament, and Kagan himself acknowledged the historical lineage.

#### 4. Rothbart's Model (1981, 2007)
**Mary Rothbart** developed a comprehensive model of temperament centred on two broad constructs:
- **Reactivity** (the excitability of motor, affective, autonomic, and endocrine systems)
- **Self-regulation** (particularly **effortful control** — the capacity to inhibit a dominant response in favour of a subdominant one)

Rothbart's model, operationalised through instruments such as the **Children's Behaviour Questionnaire (CBQ)** and the **Adult Temperament Questionnaire (ATQ)**, represents the most empirically developed contemporary temperament framework. While not organised around the classical four types, it captures many of the same individual differences in biologically-grounded personality variation.

#### 5. Cloninger's Psychobiological Model (1987, 1993)
**C. Robert Cloninger** proposed a biosocial model distinguishing four dimensions of **temperament** (heritable, biologically-based) from three dimensions of **character** (influenced by learning and maturation):

Temperament dimensions:
- **Novelty Seeking** (associated with dopaminergic activity)
- **Harm Avoidance** (associated with serotonergic activity)
- **Reward Dependence** (associated with noradrenergic activity)
- **Persistence** (later separated from reward dependence)

These dimensions can be seen as capturing elements of the classical temperaments (e.g., high novelty seeking ≈ sanguine/choleric; high harm avoidance ≈ melancholic), though the mapping is imperfect.

### D. The Big Five / Five-Factor Model and the Temperaments

The dominant contemporary framework in personality psychology — the **Big Five** or **Five-Factor Model (FFM)** of personality (Costa & McCrae, 1992) — identifies five broad traits:

1. **Openness to Experience (O)**
2. **Conscientiousness (C)**
3. **Extraversion (E)**
4. **Agreeableness (A)**
5. **Neuroticism (N)**

The relationship to the four temperaments can be approximately sketched as follows:

| Temperament | FFM Profile |
|---|---|
| **Sanguine** | High E, Low N, Moderate-High O, Low-Moderate C |
| **Choleric** | Moderate-High E, High N (or moderate N with high assertiveness), Low A, Moderate C |
| **Melancholic** | Low E, High N, High C, Variable O |
| **Phlegmatic** | Low E, Low N, High A, Moderate C |

However, this mapping is necessarily imprecise because:
- The FFM is **five-dimensional** and the temperament system is **two-dimensional** (or at most a 2×2 categorical matrix)
- Openness, Conscientiousness, and Agreeableness have no straightforward analogues in the classical system
- The FFM is explicitly **dimensional** and continuous, while the temperaments were originally **categorical**

## IX. LATER APPLIED AND POPULAR TYPOLOGIES DERIVED FROM THE FOUR TEMPERAMENTS

### A. Keirsey Temperament Sorter

**David Keirsey** (1921–2013), in *Please Understand Me* (1978) and *Please Understand Me II* (1998), proposed a fourfold temperament classification that drew upon both the classical temperaments and **Jungian type theory** (as operationalised in the **Myers-Briggs Type Indicator**):

| Keirsey Temperament | Classical Analogue | MBTI Groups |
|---|---|---|
| **Artisan (SP)** | Sanguine | ESTP, ESFP, ISTP, ISFP |
| **Guardian (SJ)** | Melancholic | ESTJ, ESFJ, ISTJ, ISFJ |
| **Idealist (NF)** | Choleric (modified) | ENFJ, ENFP, INFJ, INFP |
| **Rational (NT)** | Phlegmatic (modified) | ENTJ, ENTP, INTJ, INTP |

Keirsey's system is widely used in applied settings (education, career counselling, organisational development) but is viewed with considerable scepticism in academic personality psychology due to methodological concerns about the MBTI and the categorical nature of the typology.

### B. DISC Model

The **DISC** personality assessment system (developed from the work of **William Moulton Marston**, *Emotions of Normal People*, 1928) classifies individuals along four behavioural dimensions:
- **Dominance** (≈ Choleric)
- **Influence** (≈ Sanguine)
- **Steadiness** (≈ Phlegmatic)
- **Conscientiousness** (≈ Melancholic)

### C. Christian Pastoral Applications

The four temperaments have been extensively adopted in **Christian pastoral psychology and spiritual direction**, notably by:
- **Conrad Hock** (*The Four Temperaments*, early 20th century)
- **Fr. Jordan Aumann** and other spiritual writers
- **Tim LaHaye** (*Spirit-Controlled Temperament*, 1966; *Why You Act the Way You Do*, 1984) — a popular evangelical treatment

These applications typically argue that:
1. God creates individuals with particular temperaments as part of their nature
2. Each temperament has characteristic strengths and weaknesses
3. Spiritual maturity involves the sanctification and disciplining of one's temperament through grace
4. Understanding temperaments aids pastoral care, marriage counselling, and spiritual direction

### D. Steiner/Waldorf Education

**Rudolf Steiner** (1861–1925) incorporated the four temperaments into the pedagogical philosophy of **Waldorf education**, arguing that teachers should recognise children's temperamental types and adapt their teaching methods accordingly. In Steiner's anthroposophical framework, the temperaments are associated with different "members" of the human being (physical body, etheric body, astral body, ego).

## X. CRITICAL EVALUATION

### A. Strengths and Enduring Value

1. **Phenomenological validity**: The four temperaments capture genuine and widely recognised patterns of individual difference. The observation that some people are constitutionally more emotionally reactive, socially oriented, energetic, or cautious is empirically well-supported.

2. **Parsimony**: The fourfold scheme provides a simple, memorable, and practically useful heuristic for understanding personality variation.

3. **Integration of biology and psychology**: The temperament tradition anticipated, in broad outline, modern biopsychological approaches to personality that ground individual differences in neurobiological mechanisms.

4. **Cross-cultural resonance**: Similar fourfold or multi-type personality classifications appear in other traditions (e.g., Indian Ayurvedic medicine's *tridosha* system of *vata*, *pitta*, *kapha*; Chinese medicine's five-element theory), suggesting that the impulse to classify temperamental variation is deeply rooted.

5. **Historical-cultural significance**: As a framework that shaped medical practice, literary characterisation, artistic representation, philosophical anthropology, and everyday self-understanding for over two millennia, the four temperaments constitute an indispensable object of study for the history of ideas.

### B. Limitations and Criticisms

1. **Falsified physiological basis**: The humoral theory upon which the temperaments were originally grounded is entirely false. There is no "black bile," and bodily fluids do not determine personality in the manner Galen described.

2. **Typological vs. dimensional**: Modern personality psychology strongly favours **dimensional** models (continuous traits) over **typological** models (discrete categories). Factor-analytic research consistently shows that personality variables are continuously distributed in the population rather than clustering into distinct types (though some recent latent class analyses have identified personality "types," these remain controversial and are not isomorphic with the classical four). The Barnum effect and confirmation bias may explain much of the subjective sense that people fit neatly into temperamental categories.

3. **Overly simplistic**: Four categories are insufficient to capture the full complexity of human personality. The Big Five model, with its five broad factors and thirty facets, provides far greater descriptive adequacy. Even Eysenck's two-factor model, while mapping onto the four temperaments, was criticised for being too narrow (hence his later addition of Psychoticism).

4. **Lack of predictive specificity**: Broad temperamental categories generate vague predictions that are difficult to falsify. Modern trait psychology aims for specific, testable predictions about behaviour in defined situations.

5. **Nature-nurture oversimplification**: The classical temperament tradition was heavily **nativist**, treating temperament as constitutionally fixed. Modern developmental science emphasises the complex interaction between genetic predisposition and environmental influence (gene-environment interaction, epigenetics, differential susceptibility), rendering simple constitutional determinism untenable.

6. **Cultural and historical variability**: The specific characterisations of the temperaments reflect the values, assumptions, and social structures of the cultures that produced them. The association of melancholy with genius, for instance, is arguably a cultural construction rather than an empirical generalisation. Similarly, the gendering of temperaments (women traditionally considered cold and moist, hence phlegmatic) reflects patriarchal assumptions.

## XI. PHILOSOPHICAL AND EPISTEMOLOGICAL REFLECTIONS

The four temperaments raise several enduring philosophical questions:

1. **The type-trait problem**: Is personality better described by **categories** (types) or **dimensions** (traits)? This remains an active debate in personality psychology, though the dimensional view currently predominates.

2. **Biological determinism and moral agency**: To what extent does constitutional temperament constrain free will and moral responsibility? This question, posed by Galen, debated by Aquinas, and still alive in contemporary neuroethics, is fundamental to moral philosophy.

3. **The relationship between body and mind**: The temperament tradition embodies a form of **psychosomatic holism** that, despite its specific errors, anticipates contemporary emphasis on embodied cognition, the gut-brain axis, and psychoneuroimmunology.

4. **The epistemology of classification**: The history of the four temperaments illustrates how classificatory systems can persist not because they are empirically adequate but because they are cognitively satisfying, culturally embedded, and practically useful — raising questions about the relationship between scientific taxonomy and the natural kinds (or conventional kinds) it purports to describe.

---

## XII. CONCLUSION

The four temperaments — sanguine, choleric, melancholic, and phlegmatic — constitute one of Western civilisation's most enduring and influential frameworks for understanding human personality. Originating in Greek natural philosophy and medicine, elaborated through Roman, Islamic, and medieval European intellectual traditions, reinterpreted through Renaissance humanism and Enlightenment philosophy, and partially vindicated by modern biological personality psychology, the temperaments have demonstrated remarkable resilience.

Their persistence is not accidental. While the specific humoral theory that generated them is false, the underlying insight — that human beings differ from one another in systematic, biologically grounded ways along dimensions of emotional reactivity, sociability, energy, and behavioural regulation — has been amply confirmed by modern research. The two-dimensional structure revealed by Eysenck (Extraversion × Neuroticism) reproduces the fourfold classification with surprising fidelity, suggesting that the ancient observers, despite their erroneous causal theories, were remarkably accurate phenomenologists.

At the same time, the temperaments serve as a cautionary example of the seductions of typological thinking — the human tendency to impose neat categorical boundaries on what is, in reality, a continuous and multidimensional landscape of individual variation. The history of the four temperaments is therefore simultaneously a history of enduring psychological insight and a history of the limitations of pre-scientific reasoning — and it remains instructive for both reasons.
